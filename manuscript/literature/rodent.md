# Rodent breathing: literature survey

Survey for the zephyr methods manuscript, written 2026-10-19. The paper infers breathing (waveform and inhalation onsets) from orofacial video of head-fixed mice and validates it against a synchronized intranasal thermistor (560 sessions, 50 mice).

**How references were verified.** Every reference with a PMID was retrieved from PubMed, which supplied the authors, title, journal, year and DOI. Quantitative statements were read in the full text or abstract, as noted. Items checked only on the web are marked (web), and anything not confirmed is marked UNVERIFIED.

---

## 1. Why breathing matters in neuroscience

Sniffing is the motor act that samples odours. Rats can discriminate odours within a single sniff (Uchida & Mainen 2003), and the sniff has been called a unit of olfactory processing (Kepecs et al. 2006). Sniffing actively shapes olfactory bulb input (Verhagen et al. 2007; Wachowiak 2011). Mitral/tufted cell responses are locked to precise latencies within the sniff cycle (Shusterman et al. 2011), so inhalation **onset** is the reference event for olfactory coding. That is the reason for scoring onsets and not only rate.

Breathing also paces other orofacial actions. A medullary rhythm generator, the preBötzinger complex (Smith et al. 1991), resets whisking at each inspiration (Moore et al. 2013; Kleinfeld et al. 2014). Nose and head movements are phase-locked to the sniff (Kurnikova et al. 2017), and this coupling changes with behavioural state (Liao & Kleinfeld 2023).

Beyond the orofacial system, nasal respiration entrains oscillations across the brain (Tort et al. 2018; Heck et al. 2017). Examples include barrel cortex delta oscillations and gamma power (Ito et al. 2014), cortico-hippocampal dynamics during offline states (Karalis & Sirota 2022), and prefrontal assemblies, where the coupling depends on behavioural state (Folschweiller & Sauer 2022, 2023). Respiratory pauses mark sleep micro-architecture (Casali et al. 2026).

Together these papers justify treating breathing as a variable to record in any awake-mouse experiment, not as a nuisance signal.

## 2. How rodent breathing is measured now

The standard review is Grimaud & Murthy (2018). Every chronic method with millisecond onset precision enters the nose or the body:

- **Intranasal thermistor or thermocouple** (Liu & Han 2022 protocol; Findley et al. 2021). The original approach punctures the nasal roof. McAfee et al. (2016) placed the probe in a cavity above the nasal epithelium. Against a chest piezo in 3 head-fixed mice, that probe detected inspiration at +5.4 ± 8.9 ms, whereas an extranasal thermistor at the nostril was −40.3 ± 27.4 ms early and lost cycles during sniffing.
- **Intranasal pressure cannula** (Wesson et al. 2008; Janke et al. 2023). This is the onset reference used to validate thermistors and thermography. Findley et al. (2021) compared thermistor and cannula directly, but the size of the lag was not extracted here.
- **Whole-body or barometric plethysmography** (Hegoburu et al. 2011; Receno et al. 2019; Jessberger et al. 2016). It is non-invasive but confines the animal to a small chamber. Head-out and double-chamber variants exist (Mailhot-Larouche et al. 2018).
- **EMG of respiratory muscles.** It needs abdominal surgery; implants last weeks to months (Grimaud & Murthy 2018).
- **Telemetric thoracic or jugular pressure.** The sensor is implanted in the thorax (Reisert et al. 2014) or the jugular vein (Dasgupta et al. 2024; Kushwaha & Dasgupta 2025). Recording lasts for weeks without a tether, but McAfee et al. report that onset precision degrades at low breathing rates.
- **Piezo chest strips and electric-field sensors** (Noble et al. 2017; Zeng et al. 2012). Non-invasive, but they need a still animal.
- **Infrared thermography** of the nose in head-restrained mice (Esquivelzeta Rabell et al. 2017; Mutlu et al. 2018). This is discussed in Section 4.

Grimaud & Murthy summarise video monitoring as working only "for short periods of time (seconds to minutes)".

## 3. Animal welfare and reliability of implanted sensors

**What can be claimed with citations.**

- *Tissue damage and stress.* Sensors that enter the nasal cavity obstruct airflow and irritate or damage the epithelium. Cannulae clog with mucus or blood and must be cleared by pressure, suction or a wire, which McAfee et al. (2016) call "additional stressors". They also load the nose and the weak mouse neck. Grimaud & Murthy (2018) add that cannulae must be cleaned daily or regrown tissue clogs them, and that the surgery "can interfere with the way the rodents breathe and perceive odors, even though this has never been investigated". Older nasal-cavity placements needed "days or weeks of recovery" (McAfee et al. 2016).
- *Signal degradation.* Rafilson et al. (bioRxiv 2024 v2; *Chem Senses* 2025) state that chronic methods "are invasive, and none are perfectly robust". The bioRxiv text adds that thermistors suffer "signal degradation in recordings spanning weeks or months" and that intranasal sensors are "prone to blockage". Their motivation was to replace the sensor with bulb LFPs, and they found that onset timing could not be recovered reliably because LFP-to-breath delays differ between animals.
- *Sleep and saturation.* Intranasal thermal signals are attenuated during sleep, which motivated pressure-based recording in Casali et al. (2026) (web summary; not checked in the full text).
- *Data loss.* The keypoint-MoSeq thermistor cohort removed recordings with "excessive noise, unusually high breathing rates (>14 Hz), or unusual autocorrelation profiles" after manual inspection (Weinreb et al. 2024, full text). Thermistor data loss is real but rarely reported as a rate.
- *Surgery and housing.* Analgesia for craniotomy is under-reported (King et al. 2023). Implanted rodents are traditionally single-housed (Pinnell et al. 2016; Evers et al. 2023). Multimodal analgesia measurably changes welfare scores after skull implants (Munk et al. 2024). Severity categories and assessment are set out in Tappe-Theodor et al. (2022) and Hohlbaum et al. (2018), and the Mouse Grimace Scale is a standard pain measure (Langford et al. 2010). Head fixation alone raises corticosterone, which returns to control levels over days (Juczewski et al. 2020). A video method still needs head fixation, so the saving is the nasal implant and its upkeep, not the restraint.
- *3Rs framing.* Refinement as defined by Russell & Burch and the NC3Rs (Tannenbaum & Bennett 2015; Prescott & Lidster 2017). ARRIVE 2.0 for reporting (Percie du Sert et al. 2020). An NC3Rs working group gives guidance on recording implants (Lidster et al. 2016). Breuer et al. (2023) explicitly frame camera respiration monitoring as 3Rs refinement.

**Gap.** No study was found that reports thermistor or cannula failure rates over weeks, or that measures the welfare cost of nasal implants specifically. The paper's own 50-mouse cohort, with its implant attrition and signal loss over sessions, may be the best quantitative evidence available and is worth reporting.

## 4. Video-based rodent breathing and orofacial analysis

Each entry below records what was validated, against what reference, with which metric, and at what level.

- **Orofacial trackers without a respiratory reference.** Facemap tracks four nose keypoints to "capture sniffing" and predicts neural activity, but it has no respiratory ground truth. Its tracker "had variable performance on videos from other labs" (Syeda et al. 2024, full text). Stringer et al. (2019) and Musall et al. (2019) use motion energy and SVD of the face as predictors of neural activity, not as respiration. DeepLabCut, SLEAP and Dolensek et al. (2020) are general pose or expression tools.
- **face-rhythm.** The brief guessed "Hira/Akiyama" or the Pachitariu group. It is in fact Hakim, Jaggi, Heo, …, Musall & Sabatini (bioRxiv 2025, PMID 42079191). It recovers "sniffing" components without supervision, validated by decoding task variables and neural activity. No respiratory sensor is mentioned in the abstract; the full text was not available through PMC.
- **Video with a sensor, but alignment only (no inference).** Keypoint-MoSeq recorded thermistors in 5 mice (9 sessions) with 120 Hz 3D keypoints. It found weak, respiration-synchronized nose velocity and "sniff-aligned" syllables, scored by the probability of inhalation within ±50 ms of syllable onset (Weinreb et al. 2024). Kurnikova et al. (2017) related nose motion to thermocouple onsets in 3 head-restrained rats. At 2–4 Hz the nose shows extra peaks between breaths, evidence of a separate nose oscillator. This supports the paper's argument that orofacial motion is not a 1:1 breathing proxy. Findley et al. (2021) and Gao et al. (2026) track nose or head kinematics against thermistor or pressure in freely moving mice. All three measure coupling; none predicts breathing.
- **Closest counterexample: IR thermography, head-restrained mice.** Esquivelzeta Rabell et al. (2017) validated nasal IR thermography against simultaneous nasal pressure and report "highly reliable detection of inhalation onsets". Mutlu et al. (2018) removed the need for segmentation and say the method "reliably detects inhalation onsets with high temporal precision". Grimaud & Murthy cite a lag of about 3 ms versus cannula. This is **onset-level validation against an implanted reference in head-restrained mice**. Its limits: a thermal camera rather than ordinary video; per-animal signal processing with no learned model, so held-out animals are not an issue; few mice; and the exact metrics were not seen here (full texts paywalled; UNVERIFIED).
- **Rate-level camera methods.** Breuer et al. (2023): RGB video of the thorax in unrestrained rats against an ECG-derived rate, 5.46% relative error. Geuther et al. (2022): video features that track breathing rate during sleep, in 16 mice, "genetically validated" with no airflow reference. Svetlove et al. (2022): paper markers on the chest, compared with X-ray lung function. Lauridsen et al. (2019): Eulerian magnification. Kawabe et al. (2024): laser and radar in anaesthetized rats, with video as the rate reference. None works at the onset or waveform level against airflow.

**Conclusion for the novelty claim.** No paper was found that trains a model on ordinary (visible or near-IR) orofacial video, outputs a waveform and inhalation onsets, and scores it against a synchronized intranasal thermistor or cannula on held-out animals with a quantified onset metric. The claim holds if it is restricted to "conventional video", "learned models" and "held-out animals". IR thermography (Haesler lab) must be cited and distinguished, because it is a non-contact onset-level method validated against nasal pressure.

## 5. Open datasets and benchmarks

No benchmark exists for rodent breathing from video. Several public DANDI datasets do pair breathing with video or tracking, all in **freely moving** animals with overhead or arena cameras rather than orofacial close-ups (checked through the DANDI API):

- DANDI 000540: Liao & Kleinfeld 2023, rats, 33 subjects, "Videography; Respiration; EMG", CC-BY-4.0.
- DANDI 000217: Findley et al. 2021, 25 mice, movement tracking plus sniff recordings, CC0.
- DANDI 001433 and 001252: Smear lab, 4 mice, sniffing plus video plus bulb electrophysiology, CC-BY-4.0.

Face-video resources without respiration include the Facemap keypoint data, IBL video (figshare record 19694452; DOI not confirmed) and the human rPPG benchmarks (see the human survey). The defensible claim is therefore: **no public dataset of head-fixed orofacial video paired with synchronized intranasal thermistry at scale (hundreds of sessions, tens of animals) with an animal-held-out split.** The flat claim "no public paired dataset exists" would be false.

- Liao SM, Kleinfeld D (2023). A change in behavioral state switches the pattern of motor output that underlies rhythmic head and orofacial movements. *Curr Biol* 33:1951. DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2023.04.008, PMID 37105167. Its data are DANDI 000540 (video plus respiration, rats).
- Findley et al. (2021), above. Data at DANDI 000217.
- Sterrett SC, Findley TM, Rafilson SE, et al., Smear MC (2024). Olfactory bulb tracks breathing rhythms and place in freely behaving mice. *bioRxiv*. DOI 10.1101/2024.11.06.622362, PMID 39574737. Likely matches DANDI 001252/001433 (the dandiset names match the paper; the link is not confirmed).

## Gaps and threats to the novelty claim

1. **IR thermography (Esquivelzeta Rabell et al. 2017; Mutlu et al. 2018)** is a non-contact, onset-level method validated against nasal pressure in head-restrained mice. Reviewers will cite it. Distinguish on: an ordinary camera instead of a thermal one; a waveform output in addition to onsets; a learned model tested on held-out animals; scale (50 mice, 560 sessions against a few); and a public dataset. The full-text metrics were not retrieved, so obtain the PDFs and report their numbers fairly.
2. **Paired public data exist** (DANDI 000540, 000217, 001433), though freely moving and not orofacial. Narrow the dataset claim accordingly.
3. **Sensor-aligned video work** (keypoint-MoSeq, Kurnikova, Findley, Moore) shows that face and nose motion carry breath timing. A reviewer may argue that predictability follows from this. Answer with the paper's own result that motion energy and SVD-plus-ridge baselines fail on new animals, and with Kurnikova's breath-independent nose peaks.
4. **face-rhythm (2025)** and Facemap label "sniffing" components from video. Verify in the face-rhythm full text that no respiratory sensor was used.
5. **Welfare evidence is mostly qualitative** (McAfee; Grimaud & Murthy; Rafilson). No failure-rate statistics for nasal implants were found. Report the cohort's own implant attrition and signal loss to fill this gap.
6. **Not searched exhaustively:** IEEE/arXiv engineering literature on animal video vital signs; non-English sources; conference abstracts (SfN, AChemS). A bioRxiv-native search tool was unavailable, so bioRxiv was covered only through web search and PubMed preprint indexing.
